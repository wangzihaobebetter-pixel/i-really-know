# Patient write-up: 58-year-old man with acute chest pain

**Course:** MED 540 — Clinical Reasoning II  
**Patient:** Mr. R (initials changed), age 58  
**Encounter date:** 12 February 2026  
**Setting:** Emergency Department, academic medical center

## Chief complaint

"I feel like there's an elephant sitting on my chest."

## History of present illness

Mr. R is a 58-year-old man with a history of hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, and a 30 pack-year smoking history who presents to the ED with substernal chest pressure that began approximately 90 minutes prior to arrival, while he was watching television. He describes the pain as a heavy pressure, 8/10 in severity, non-radiating, associated with diaphoresis and mild dyspnoea. He denies nausea, vomiting, palpitations, syncope, fever, cough, leg pain or swelling, and recent travel. The pain did not improve with rest or with two sublingual nitroglycerin tablets given by EMS en route. He has not previously had chest pain like this.

## Past medical history

- Hypertension (diagnosed ~2015, on lisinopril)
- Hyperlipidaemia (on atorvastatin 40 mg)
- No prior MI, stroke, or DVT/PE
- No known coronary disease

## Medications

- Lisinopril 20 mg daily
- Atorvastatin 40 mg nightly
- Aspirin 81 mg daily (started by PCP last year for primary prevention)

## Allergies

No known drug allergies.

## Family history

Father had MI at age 62; mother with type 2 diabetes.

## Social history

30 pack-year smoker, currently 1 pack/day. Alcohol 2–3 drinks/week. No illicit drug use. Works as an electrician. Married, two adult children.

## Vitals

- BP 158/94 mmHg (right arm)
- HR 96 bpm, regular
- RR 18 /min
- SpO₂ 96% on room air
- Temperature 37.0 °C oral
- Weight 88 kg, height 178 cm

## Physical exam

General: diaphoretic, in moderate distress, clutching chest. Lungs: clear to auscultation bilaterally, no wheeze. Cardiovascular: JVP not elevated; regular rate and rhythm; no murmurs, rubs, or gallops; pulses 2+ and symmetric in all four extremities. Abdomen: soft, non-tender. Extremities: no oedema, no calf tenderness, no asymmetric swelling. Neurological: alert, oriented ×3, no focal deficit.

## Labs and studies

- **ECG (initial):** Sinus rhythm at 92 bpm. ST-segment elevation of 1.5 mm in leads II, III, and aVF, with reciprocal ST depression of 0.5–1 mm in leads I and aVL. No pathologic Q waves. Right-sided leads not yet obtained.
- **Troponin I (initial, ~90 min after pain onset):** 0.42 ng/mL (assay upper reference 0.04 ng/mL).
- **CBC:** WBC 9.8 × 10⁹/L, Hb 14.1 g/dL, platelets 218 × 10⁹/L.
- **BMP:** Na 139, K 4.1, Cl 102, HCO₃ 24, BUN 16, Cr 1.0, glucose 122.
- **D-dimer:** not ordered yet (see assessment).
- **Chest X-ray:** no pneumothorax, no widened mediastinum, no consolidation, normal cardiac silhouette.

## Assessment

Mr. R is a 58-year-old man with multiple cardiovascular risk factors presenting with acute substernal chest pressure, dynamic inferior ST elevation on ECG, and an elevated troponin. The leading diagnosis is **inferior ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI)**, most likely involving the right coronary artery territory given the inferior lead pattern and reciprocal changes in I/aVL.

Differential considered:

1. **Inferior STEMI (RCA vs dominant LCx).** Supported by inferior ST elevation, reciprocal changes, troponin elevation, and risk-factor profile. The absence of RV involvement has not been confirmed because right-sided leads were not yet obtained; will obtain V4R at the bedside.
2. **Acute pulmonary embolism.** Less likely given the focal inferior ST elevation, which is not the typical pattern for PE (PE more commonly produces S1Q3T3 or diffuse T-wave inversion). However, the smoking history makes this non-zero, and lower-extremity Doppler will be obtained if cath is negative.
3. **Aortic dissection (type A involving RCA ostium).** Considered because dissection can cause secondary ischaemia. The chest X-ray shows no mediastinal widening, and pulses are symmetric, making it less likely, but the patient will get CT angiography of the aorta if the cath-lab activation turns out to be inappropriate.
4. **Pericarditis.** Unlikely given the focal regional ST elevation with reciprocal changes rather than diffuse concave-up elevation, and the lack of pericardial rub or pleuritic features.
5. **Esophageal spasm / GERD.** A non-cardiac cause is on the differential but is a diagnosis of exclusion given the ECG and troponin findings; this workup would only proceed after cardiac aetiologies are excluded.

The patient meets criteria for activation of the cath lab for primary PCI.

## Management plan

1. **Cath-lab activation** for primary PCI. Aspirin 325 mg chewed already given by EMS. Will give ticagrelor 180 mg PO loading dose and unfractionated heparin 70 U/kg IV bolus per protocol. Door-to-balloon target <90 minutes.
2. **Right-sided ECG (V4R)** at the bedside to evaluate for RV infarction before giving nitroglycerin or other preload-reducing agents.
3. **Nitroglycerin** sublingual or IV, contingent on V4R not showing RV infarction and BP tolerating it.
4. **Statin intensification** — switch atorvastatin to high-intensity (atorvastatin 80 mg or rosuvastatin 40 mg) regardless of baseline LDL, per secondary-prevention recommendations after ACS.
5. **Beta blocker** within 24 hours if no contraindications develop (no acute heart failure, no hypotension once RV involvement is excluded).
6. **Cardiology admission** to CCU for monitoring, serial troponins, post-PCI care, and risk-factor counselling, including smoking-cessation referral before discharge.
7. **Disposition:** admit to cardiac care unit after PCI. If cath shows non-obstructive disease, expand workup to CT aortogram and bilateral lower-extremity duplex before reconsidering the differential.
